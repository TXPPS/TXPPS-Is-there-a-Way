## The pitch

**March 1998.** A structural engineer arrives alone at a decommissioned
lock-and-dam on the Upper Mississippi to write the condition survey that will
justify demolishing it. Four hours in, the dam's automated flood-response
sequence fires on a river-gauge reading that cannot be true, and seals every
bulkhead between them and the road.

The only control that can reverse the sequence is in the tainter gate control
house, on the far side of the structure. The only route left to it runs down,
through a 1962 fallout shelter, and through the federally funded isolation
programme that used the shelter from 1964 to 1977.

Someone else is down there. He has staged food for exactly one person. He has
made one bunk and stripped the other. He taped the frayed cable in the stair
tower before the engineer ever got to it. He sealed them in, and he is certain
he is doing them a kindness.

## The place

Built 1937 under the WPA; rehabilitated 1954; lock decommissioned 1994 when
traffic moved to the 600 ft chamber at L&D 10. The gates and the powerhouse were
left operable because the dam still holds the pool.

Four levels, and the game is the descent through them.

### 1. The lock wall and powerhouse — **Act 1**

Deck level and one below. Generator hall, switchgear room, battery room, the
lockmaster's office with its landline and its wall of logbooks. The stair tower
down. Sodium bulkhead lamps, wet concrete, forty years of river silt in the low
third of every wall.

### 2. The gallery — **Act 1 into Act 2**

The service tunnel inside the dam itself, running the width of the structure
between the powerhouse and the gate piers. Drain gallery below it. This is the
only horizontal route once the sequence has fired, and it is the reason the game
goes down before it goes across.

### 3. The shelter — **Act 2**

Built 1962 as a Community Fallout Shelter under the National Fallout Shelter
Survey: capacity 140, two weeks' rations, its own well and its own generator.
Bunk rooms, mess, latrines, plant room, ration store. Ordinary, municipal,
and — because Emil has kept it — habitable.

### 4. The annex — **Act 3**

Cut into the rock behind the shelter in 1964 for the programme. Observation
corridor, three isolation chambers, the tank room, the recorder bay, the tape
library. Fluorescent, not sodium: the palette shift in `ART_BIBLE.md` is the
marker that the player has left the *dam* and entered the *programme*.

### 5. The gate piers and control house — **Act 4**

Up again, inside the dam, to the tainter gate machinery and the control house
that commands the sequence. The only place the seal can be lifted, and the only
place the river can be let in.

## The programme

This section exists because the puzzles are built out of the equipment the
programme left behind, so the equipment has to be what this purpose would
actually have required. (`STORY_NOTES.md` S2.)

### The funding line

- **Office of Civil Defense, Department of the Army — Shelter Habitability
  Research Program**, Task Order **4-A**, *"Extended Occupancy: Sensory and
  Temporal Factors"*. Appropriations **FY1964 – FY1971**.
- Continued after the OCD's 1972 reorganisation under the **Defense Civil
  Preparedness Agency** as **Work Unit 1615-C**, **FY1972 – FY1977**.
- Contracting institution: the **Ridgewater Field Station**, administered on
  paper by a university psychology department three hundred miles away that sent
  a principal investigator twice a year.
- Terminated **30 June 1977** when DCPA's habitability line was not renewed.
  Records retired to Federal Records Center, Kansas City, 1978. The hardware
  stayed, because removing it cost money the closure order did not appropriate.

### What it was actually measuring

Not "the effects of isolation". Something narrower and answerable, which is why
it got funded:

> **How long a confined group holds a shared sense of time when every exterior
> cue is removed, and what is the smallest imposed signal that restores it.**

The civil-defense question underneath is blunt: a community sealed in a shelter
for two weeks with no windows, no radio and no clock will drift out of phase
with each other and stop functioning as a group. What is the cheapest thing you
can give them that stops that happening? A siren? A voice? A light?

The programme's answer, across nine runs, was **a light on a schedule**. One
luminaire, in one place, brightening and dimming on a twenty-four hour cycle
that the subjects were never told about. Groups given the lamp re-synchronised
within four days. Groups given nothing did not.

### The method, in the programme's own words

From **Protocol 4 — Observation** (Ridgewater Field Station, rev. 1966), the
document the whole game turns on:

> *4.1 The chamber shall be dark except for the schedule luminaire.*
>
> *4.2 The observer stands at the lamp. The subject may look toward the lamp at
> any time.*
>
> *4.3 The observer shall not speak except on the schedule. The subject shall
> not be told the schedule.*
>
> *4.4 A run is not concluded until the observer leaves the lamp.*

That is the method. Everything the entity does comes out of those four lines,
and 4.4 is the whole of Act 4.

## Emil Ostrander

Born 1938, Fairport, Iowa. Hired by the Corps in 1962 as a **plant electrician**
at Ridgewater. Seconded to the Field Station in 1964 as its **facilities
technician** and stayed in that post for all nine runs.

**His job was the lamp.** He wired C-1, he cut the cams for C-2, he ran C-3, he
changed the paper on C-4 and he threaded C-8. When Protocol 4.2 says *the
observer stands at the lamp*, the observer it means, for thirteen years, was
usually Emil. The principal investigator came twice a year. Emil was there every
run, in the dark, at the light, not speaking except on the schedule.

He is not a scientist and never claimed to be. He is a competent, careful,
literal man who was given a procedure and followed it exactly, for thirteen
years, and was never once told what it was for.

After 1977 he stayed on as the dam's electrician. After the 1994
decommissioning his paycheques stopped and his keys did not. He has lived in the
shelter for four years. He is sixty. He is not mad in any way that shows: he is
a man running the only procedure he has.

### What he believes

1. Run 9 was terminated early, on 12 May 1977, when the subject was brought up
   before the observer left the lamp. **Protocol 4.4 was violated.**
2. Everything since is the consequence. The run never concluded, so the observer
   never left, so something is still at the lamp.
3. A sealed shelter with a controlled light is the only place he knows how to
   keep a person alive, because it is the only place he has ever kept anyone
   alive.
4. Therefore: seal the door, stage the food, keep one bunk made, tape the cable,
   and wait. **Outside is worse.**

He is not wrong about the danger. He is wrong about the cure.

### How his kindness is legible from the first room (S1)

Before any tape, any note, any voice. This is a design obligation on P5, not a
description of a cut scene:

- **Act 1, powerhouse:** the frayed conduit in the stair tower is taped, neatly,
  with new tape. A hand-lettered card reads `WATCH THE THIRD STEP` in engineer's
  capitals. Somebody has been maintaining a building that was abandoned in 1994.
- **Act 1, lockmaster's office:** the logbook has entries after the
  decommissioning, in one hand, in pencil, recording nothing but the river stage
  and the plant hours. Somebody has been keeping the log.
- **Act 2, mess:** fourteen days of rations laid out for one person, dated,
  rotated, oldest first. Not a hoard. A *schedule*.
- **Act 2, bunk room:** one bunk made with hospital corners. The bunk beside it
  is stripped to the springs and the mattress is rolled at the foot. Not two
  people. One person, and a place kept for one more.
- **Act 2, plant room:** the generator's service card is filled in monthly since
  1994. The last entry is this month.
- **Act 3, corridor:** the barricade across the flooded stair is built, not
  fallen. Someone measured the timber.

A player who quits at the 40% mark should already be uneasy about who has been
tidying up. That is the S1 test, and the rooms above are how it is passed.

## The rule

The one thing the player must be able to learn, state, and act on.

> **The observer stands at the lamp.**

That is Protocol 4.2, it is a line in a document the player can find, and it is
the entity's entire behaviour. From it, everything follows:

| The rule | What the player sees | What the player can do about it |
|---|---|---|
| It occupies the space between a lit fixture and you | A vertical seam of light interrupted for the width of a body, then restored | Nothing yet. This is the first lesson: the seam is not a glitch. |
| It can only approach along that axis | The seam appears on the lamp–you line and nowhere else | **Step off the line.** The approach breaks. |
| It cannot cross unlit space | In full darkness nothing comes | Kill the lamp — and lose the ability to move safely, and lose the progress that lamp was powering |
| Two lit fixtures from different angles leave no offset | Two seams, converging | **More light is not more safety.** Light one, not both. |
| A run concludes when the observer leaves the lamp | — | Act 4 |

This is why restoring power is both the win condition and the risk, which is
what `ART_BIBLE.md` already asserted and this document now earns.

**The S3 test:** a player who has read the documents can state the rule in one
sentence — *the observer stands at the lamp* — and that sentence is a line in a
programme document. Passed.

### What it is not

It is never fully shown. It has no face, no scream, and no chase sequence. It
does not open doors. It does not follow you into the dark, because it cannot.
Its only expression is the interruption of light, and the game never gives it
another. See `ART_BIBLE.md`, "Never do this".

### The gauge

The spoof is mechanical and the player can find it. The river stage transmitter
is a float-and-tape gauge in a stilling well on the upstream pier; its reading
reaches the control house as a **selsyn** repeater. Emil disconnected the float
and drove the transmitter's selsyn from a bench unit in the gallery. The
sequence is real, the logic is sound, and it is being fed a lie by a box on a
shelf with a hand-lettered label.

Finding that box is the moment the player stops being afraid of the building and
starts being afraid of the person.

## Why every locked door is locked

No door in this game is locked "because it is a game". Each one has a mechanism,
and each mechanism is the puzzle.

| Door | Why it is shut | How it opens |
|---|---|---|
| Stair tower to deck (Act 1) | Flood-response bulkhead. Fails closed on loss of the permissive from the control house. | Only from the control house. This is the game. |
| Switchgear room | Not locked. Interlocked: the door releases when the bus is dead, which is a safety feature and an obstacle. | Kill the bus at the battery room. |
| Gallery watertight door | Manual dogging, eight dogs, one seized. | Free the dog. Mechanical, not a key. |
| Shelter blast door | Designed to be closable from inside by one person. Emil closed it. | The interlock cabinet C-7 — from inside, which is where you are going. |
| Ration store | Padlocked, and the key is on a nail beside it. Emil is not hiding food; he is keeping rodents out. | Take the key. The point is that it opens easily. |
| Annex door | Welded shut in 1978 by a contractor paid for one day. The weld is bad and Emil cut it in 1979. | Already open. The horror is that it is open. |
| Chamber A / B / C | Interlock cabinet, three channels, key-released per Protocol 4.4. | The programme's own procedure, followed correctly. |
| Tank room | Water. The room is flooded to the sill because the sump stopped when the bus dropped. | Restore the sump, which needs three-phase, which needs Act 2. |
| Gate pier stair | Flood-response bulkhead, same as the stair tower. | Control house permissive. |
| Control house | Corps padlock. Merrick has the site key ring. | You have had the key since 10:15. |

That last row is the design principle of the whole game stated once: **the door
you need was never locked; the route to it was.**

## The acts

### Act 1 — The Powerhouse *(the vertical slice, P5)*

Arrival, the exterior survey, the sequence firing, and the descent. Restore
lighting to the powerhouse from the battery bank; find the shelter door.

Ends on the shelter blast door swinging open from the inside.

**Emotional job:** establish competence. The player is good at this building.
Nothing supernatural happens. The only wrong note is that somebody has been
tidying up.

### Act 2 — The Shelter

Domestic, and worse for it. Restore three-phase from the shelter's own
generator, which means the switchgear, which means the battery room, which means
understanding the plant. Emil speaks for the first time, on the intercom, on a
schedule.

Ends when the annex door is found already open.

**Emotional job:** the kindness accretes past the point of comfort.

**First seam:** at the very end of Act 2, once and only once, in a corridor the
player has already walked. No sound. No consequence.

### Act 3 — The Annex

The chambers, the tank, the recorders, the tape library. The rule is stated in
`D-13` and demonstrated by the room. The entity is active and the player learns
to manage light rather than flee.

Ends with Reel 9-C in hand and the tank room drained.

**Emotional job:** the horror stops being about the building and becomes about
the procedure — and then about the man who performed it.

### Act 4 — The Gate

Up through the gate piers to the control house. The selsyn bench unit. Emil, in
person, once. The choice.

**Emotional job:** the player has to decide what the last twenty-one years were.

## Plot hole audit

Every answer here is mechanical. Any answer that rested on "just because" was
rewritten until it did not.

**1. Why is the player here?**
Contracted to write the pre-demolition condition survey. It is a paid,
scheduled, ordinary job with a work order number, and it requires going into
every space in the structure. The premise's central strength: the protagonist is
*supposed* to open panels and go below grade.

**2. How did they get in?**
The Corps site key ring, signed out at the Rock Island District office on 18
March, against work order `RI-98-0211`. They signed the site book at 10:15.

**3. Why can't they leave?**
The 1954 flood-response sequence closes every bulkhead between the powerhouse
and the deck and holds them closed. Those bulkheads fail *closed* on loss of the
control-house permissive — which is correct engineering, because a flood
sequence that fails open drowns the powerhouse. The permissive can be restored
from exactly one place: the control house. The route to the control house runs
down and across. There is no second exit, because a 1937 dam does not have one.

**4. Who sealed them in, and what did that person want?**
Emil Ostrander, by feeding a false stage to the gauge repeater from a bench unit
he built. What he wants is to keep a person alive in the one place he knows how
to keep a person alive. He is running Protocol 4 because it is the only
procedure he has, and Protocol 4 requires a sealed chamber, a controlled lamp,
and an observer who does not leave.

**5. Why is help not coming?**
The site is de-staffed. The survey is booked for three days; the next contact is
the Friday check-in with Averill & Pratt — thirty-six hours away and, by then,
over. The office landline in the lockmaster's room runs through a junction box
in the gallery that Emil pulled in 1996 because the ringer woke him. The
handheld radio is charging on the truck tailgate, ninety feet up and one sealed
stair tower away. Nobody is looking for a man at a dam that closed in 1994.

**6. Why has this place never been found?**
It was never lost. The shelter is on the drawings and in the county civil
defense inventory. The annex is documented as the "Occupancy Study Annex" and
its closure is a matter of federal record. The records are in Kansas City, filed
correctly, under a work unit number nobody has had a reason to pull since 1978.
There is nothing anomalous to find: a closed shelter under a closed dam, and one
retired electrician who stayed in the river town he had always lived in.

**7. Why does the player go deeper rather than wait?**
Three reasons, in the order the game makes them true:
- The route to the only control that lifts the seal runs downward. Waiting does
  not open a door.
- The sumps stopped when the bus dropped. The lower gallery is taking water and
  the level is legibly rising against a silt line the player can read. This is
  visible in Act 1 and is the game's clock.
- The powerhouse's air handling is on the surface side of the seal. The shelter
  has its own plant. Down is where the air is.

**8. What changes at the end?**
The player learns what the building was for, and either completes the procedure
that has been running since 1977 or destroys the only record that it ever ran.
Either way they leave knowing what the last line on the tape meant, which they
did not at 10:15 that morning. That is the whole arc: a competent professional
who understood a structure and now understands a procedure.

**9. Why doesn't Emil simply talk to them?**
Protocol 4.3. *The observer shall not speak except on the schedule.* He has
followed that line for thirty-four years. When he does speak, it is on the
intercom, at the scheduled hour, and it is the most frightening thing in the
game precisely because it is punctual.

## The two endings

Both are reached from the control house in Act 4. Both are earned by what the
player has done, not chosen from a menu. Both are consistent with everything
above.

### Ending A — **Conclude the run**

The player closes the last interlock channel from inside the control house,
which is what Protocol 4.4 requires: *a run is not concluded until the observer
leaves the lamp.* Concluding it means being the subject for the final
observation — standing at the lamp's line, once, deliberately, and letting the
seam close over them.

The sequence releases. The bulkheads open at dawn. Emil is at the lamp when it
goes out, and does not get up. The auditor walks up the stair tower into a grey
March morning with Reel 9-C in their jacket and a survey they will never file.

*The last shot is the deck in daylight, and one bulkhead lamp still burning
because nobody turned it off.*

### Ending B — **Open the gate**

The player refuses the procedure and does the thing an engineer would do: takes
the tainter gate off its permissive and lets the pool through the structure. The
gallery floods. The annex floods. Everything below grade is under fourteen feet
of Mississippi by first light, including the tape library, including Reel 9-C,
including Emil, who will not leave the lamp.

The auditor gets out through the gate pier stair as the water comes up behind
them. They are believed about the flood and not about anything else, because the
evidence is under the river, which is where evidence at a dam goes.

*The last shot is the same deck in the same daylight, and the water working
through the gate, and no lamp at all.*

### Why both work

Ending A is completion and costs the truth: the record survives but the player
has performed the programme's final act on themselves. Ending B is refusal and
costs the record: the player keeps their own judgement and destroys the only
proof any of it happened. Neither is the good one. Both answer the question on
the tape, and they answer it differently.

## How this world is written

Rules for every line of text in the game, so that six months of content stays in
one voice.

- **Institutional documents are boring on purpose.** A memo about a shelter
  occupancy study should read like a memo. The horror is in what a bored
  document has to say, not in how it says it.
- **Emil writes in engineer's capitals, in pencil, and never uses a word he does
  not need.** Nothing he writes is ominous. Everything he writes is correct.
- **Ketch writes formally and degrades.** Her 1966 protocol is immaculate. Her
  1974 quarterly is precise and will not say the thing it is about. Her 1977
  letter is a private sentence in a professional hand.
- **Nobody explains the entity, ever.** The closest anyone comes is Protocol
  4.2, which is not about the entity at all.
- **No document addresses the player.** No note is left for you. Everything you
  read was written for somebody else, which is why finding it is worth
  something.
- **No document may depend on column alignment.** The reader has one
  proportional font — there is no licensed monospace in this project and no
  budget for a generated one — so a four-column table does not line up. Write
  records as one line per field. On a 956-point screen that reads better anyway,
  which is the usual shape of a constraint worth having.
- **The word "kindness" never appears.** Neither does "experiment", "subject 9's
  fate", or any sentence that tells the player how to feel.
